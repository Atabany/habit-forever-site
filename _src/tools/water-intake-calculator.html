This is a starting point for healthy adults, not medical advice. Thirst is a good guide for most people: the National Academies panel wrote that “the vast majority of healthy people adequately meet their daily hydration needs by letting thirst be their guide.” If you have a health condition, read when to ask a doctor first.

The NHS keeps it simpler: aim for 6 to 8 cups or glasses of fluid a day, and it calls that “just a guide”. Water, lower-fat milk and lower-sugar drinks, including tea and coffee, all count.

How the calculator works

Drinks a day = drinks from the guideline + exercise minutes ÷ 60 × sweat rate + hot hours × 0.3 L.

  • The guideline amount is the adequate intake from the tables above. It covers a sedentary day in a mild climate.
  • Exercise. Sweat you lose has to come back from drinks. The National Academies report lists measured sweat rates of about 0.7 to 1.0 L an hour for distance runners in temperate conditions, and 1.0 to more than 2.0 L an hour for competitive runners in the heat. I use 0.3 L for light activity, 0.7 L for moderate and 1.0 L for hard exercise or exercise in the heat. Sweat rates vary a lot between people, so the American College of Sports Medicine suggests finding your own: weigh yourself before and after exercise. Each kilogram you lose is about a litre of sweat.
  • Heat. People working in desert heat sweated 0.3 to 1.2 L an hour in the studies the National Academies reviewed. I add the low end, 0.3 L an hour, for time spent working or on your feet outdoors in hot weather. Very active people who are always in hot weather often need 6 litres of total water a day or more.
  • Glasses = litres from drinks ÷ your glass size. US fluid ounces = millilitres ÷ 29.57.

When to ask a doctor

Needs vary with your body, your health and your day, and more is not always better. Talk to a doctor or nurse before changing how much you drink if:

  • you have a kidney or heart condition, or you take water tablets (diuretics). With some kidney disease, the NHS says you may be told to reduce your fluids, including the water in soups and yogurt;
  • you are pregnant or breastfeeding and unsure what applies to you;
  • you are caring for an older person: thirst weakens with age, so the National Academies set older adults’ intake from younger adults’ figures;
  • you have signs of dehydration that don’t go away, such as dark, strong-smelling pee, dizziness or unusual tiredness. The NHS lists when to get help on its dehydration page.

FAQ

How much water should I drink a day?

For healthy adults on a sedentary day in a mild climate, the US National Academies’ adequate intake works out to about 2.2 litres from drinks for women and 3.0 litres for men. The European Food Safety Authority’s total water values are lower: 2.0 litres for women and 2.5 litres for men, food included. Exercise and heat add to both.

Is 8 glasses of water a day right?

It is a reasonable rule of thumb, but not an official one. The NHS suggests 6 to 8 cups or glasses of fluid a day as a guide, and the National Academies panel said it offers no rule of thumb based on glasses.

Do tea and coffee count toward my water?

Yes. The NHS counts tea and coffee, and the National Academies panel found no convincing evidence that caffeine leads to cumulative body water deficits, so caffeinated drinks count toward your total.

Does food count?

Yes. In the National Academies’ data, about 20% of total water came from food and about 80% from drinks. That’s why the drinks figure is lower than the total water figure.

Can you drink too much water?

Yes, but it is unusual. The National Academies panel noted that people occasionally drink amounts that can be life-threatening, while calling such cases highly unusual; it set no upper limit for water. Spread drinks through the day and ask a doctor if you have a condition that affects fluids.
